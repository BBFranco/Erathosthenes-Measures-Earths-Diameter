Eratosthenes measures the Earth

Around 240 BCE, a librarian compared the shadows of two Egyptian cities and worked out how big the planet is. He landed within a few percent.

α

A well that lights up once a year

Eratosthenes ran the Library of Alexandria. Travellers told him about a well in Syene, now Aswan, far to the south. Once a year, at noon on the summer solstice, sunlight reached the water at the bottom. Nothing in town cast a shadow. The Sun was directly overhead.

The same minute, 800 km north

In Alexandria, at that same moment, an upright stick still cast a shadow. Same Sun, same minute, different result. The shadow was about one eighth as long as the stick. That means the Sun was 7.2° away from straight overhead.

γ

From a shadow to the centre of the Earth

Eratosthenes took two things as given. The Earth is round, and the Sun is so far away that its rays arrive parallel. After that, one piece of school geometry does the rest.

δ

Fifty slices

7.2° is 360° divided by 50. The distance between the two cities is one slice, so the whole Earth holds fifty of them. Eratosthenes had that distance as 5,000 stadia. Multiply by fifty and you have the circumference.

A stadion is an ancient Greek unit of length. Nobody is sure which version he used, so the answer has some play in it. Try different values.

ε

What he got wrong

His inputs were rough. The result was still close, because the method was sound.

  • The cities share no meridianAlexandria is about 3° of longitude west of Syene, so the line between them is not due north.
  • Syene was not on the tropicThe town sits slightly north of the Tropic of Cancer, so the Sun was never quite overhead.
  • The distance was an estimateNobody could measure 800 km exactly. The 5,000 stadia came from surveyors and travel times.
  • The Sun is a disc, not a dotIt spans about half a degree of sky, so shadow edges blur.
  • A stadion has no single lengthEstimates run from about 157 m to 185 m. That moves the answer by 15% or more.

With the shorter stadion he lands about 1 to 2% under the modern figure. With the longer one he overshoots by roughly 15%. Either way, a stick and a well got him in the right ballpark.

More about Eratosthenes

  • He is credited with coining the word geography.
  • He devised a sieve for finding prime numbers, still taught today.
  • Rivals reportedly nicknamed him Beta, for being second best at everything.

His own writing on the measurement is lost. The story reaches us through later authors, so details such as the stadion length and the exact distance vary between accounts.
